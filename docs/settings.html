Horizon OS Settings & Quick Settings

Redesigning how users access and customize system controls in a spatial OS — from a standalone app to an integrated Navigator experience.

Horizon OS Unity 3D C# System UI Information Architecture Navigator Integration Customization
Summary

The original Horizon OS had a single settings app — launch it, find the option, change it, close it. As the Navigator became the primary surface of the OS, the team needed a faster, more integrated way for users to access common controls without leaving their current context. I designed and prototyped Quick Settings as a sub-surface of the Navigator, and explored two architectural options for how the full Settings panel relates to it.

Beyond the IA question, I introduced a customization system that lets users choose which controls appear in their Quick Settings — with toggles in the full Settings that reflect simultaneously on the Quick Settings surface. The result is a settings experience that fits naturally into the spatial OS rather than living as a separate destination.

01

Quick Settings — Fast Access from the Navigator

Quick Settings lives as a sub-page of the Navigator, accessible from a settings tab in the sub-navigation. Users can reach Wi-Fi, brightness, volume, VR/MR mode switching, and environment controls without launching a separate app — the most common settings are always one tap away from wherever they are in the OS.

Contextual, not comprehensive. Quick Settings isn’t a miniature version of the full settings app. It surfaces only the controls users need frequently — the adjustments you make mid-session without wanting to break flow. Everything else lives in the full Settings panel, accessible from a clear entry point within Quick Settings.

Settings should come to you, not the other way around.
02

Option A — Settings Inside the Navigator

The core IA question: should the full Settings live inside Quick Settings, or outside it? I prototyped both to let the team experience the trade-offs in-headset.

Integrated model. The full Settings extends below Quick Settings as a scrollable continuation. Scroll down from your quick controls and the full settings page appears — left-side navigation, right-side content. A top arrow lets you jump back to Quick Settings. One surface, one context, no window management. The trade-off: the panel becomes long, and the mental model blurs between “quick” and “full.”

03

Option B — Settings Outside the Navigator

Independent model. Quick Settings stays compact. Tapping the Settings button opens a separate, full-size Settings panel as its own window. Clean separation — Quick Settings stays fast, full Settings gets its own space. The trade-off: an extra window to manage, and a context switch between the two.

Why both needed to be built. This wasn’t a question that could be resolved in a design review. The integrated model sounds simpler, but does it feel right when the panel gets long? The independent model sounds cleaner, but does opening a new window feel heavy in VR? Prototyping both in Unity and testing in-headset gave the team concrete answers instead of hypothetical trade-offs.

04

Customization — Your Quick Settings, Your Layout

Not every user needs the same quick controls. I designed a customization system where users can choose which settings appear in their Quick Settings panel.

Simultaneous reflection. In the full Settings under the General tab, each control has a toggle. Turn it on, and the control appears in Quick Settings immediately — no save button, no refresh. Turn it off, and it disappears. The two surfaces stay in sync in real time, so users can see exactly what their Quick Settings will look like as they configure it.

Designing for agency. Customization isn’t just a feature — it’s a signal that the OS respects how each person uses it. A gamer might want environment switching front and center. A remote worker might prioritize Wi-Fi and mic controls. The same Quick Settings surface, tailored to different workflows.

The same surface, tailored to different workflows.
Closing Thoughts

Settings sounds like a solved problem — until you redesign it for a spatial OS where launching a separate app feels like leaving the room. Moving settings into the Navigator as a sub-surface, exploring two IA models through working prototypes, and adding real-time customization turned a utilitarian feature into something that feels native to how people actually use VR.

The exploration defined how system controls should integrate with the Navigator — not as a bolted-on panel, but as a natural extension of the OS surface users already live in. Both architectural options gave the team the in-headset evidence they needed to make a confident product decision.
