Spatial UI Z-Depth Exploration

Pioneering depth as a design dimension in Horizon OS — turning flat UI into layered, spatial interfaces.

Horizon OS Unity 3D C# Spatial UI Z-Depth Design System Design Innovation
Summary

Flat UI works on a phone. In VR, it wastes the medium. I created the first z-depth prototype in the Horizon OS design system — exploring how elevation, layering, and depth-based interactions could make spatial interfaces feel more intuitive, more legible, and more alive.

This wasn’t a visual polish pass. It was a structural exploration of how depth communicates hierarchy, interactivity, and state in a 3D environment. The patterns that emerged — layered panels, lifted action bars, hover-triggered depth shifts — sparked cross-team discussions, influenced the engineering roadmap, and were adopted into premium experiences across the platform.

01

Depth as Information Architecture

In spatial computing, z-depth isn’t decoration — it’s information. I prototyped a layered UI system where each element’s elevation communicates its role in the hierarchy. The main content panel sits at the base layer. The action bar lifts forward on its own z-plane. Tooltips float above interactive elements on hover. Each layer tells the user what’s primary, what’s contextual, and what’s responsive to their input.

The design question: in a medium where everything exists in 3D space, how do you use depth to make interfaces more legible — not just more dimensional? The answer was restraint: depth should clarify relationships, not add visual noise.

Depth should clarify relationships, not add visual noise.
02

Store Panel — Z-Depth in Practice

The Horizon OS Store became the proving ground. I applied z-depth across the entire panel to test how layered elevation works in a real product surface:

Content panel. The main store page — description, included items, app thumbnails — sits on the base z-plane, establishing the primary reading surface.

Bottom action bar. The app info bar (title, rating, category, price, actions) lifts forward on its own depth layer, separating purchase decisions from content browsing. The $19.99 price button and “Free trial” sit elevated, signaling interactivity through position, not just color.

Tooltip on hover. Hovering over the wishlist icon triggers a tooltip (“Add to wishlist”) that floats above the action bar on yet another z-layer — a depth-based affordance that feels natural in 3D space, where “closer to you” means “more immediate.”

Left sidebar navigation. The About / Included Items tab navigation occupies its own depth plane, visually separating structural navigation from content. The layered separation means users can distinguish wayfinding from reading without relying on borders or heavy visual dividers — depth does the work.

Meta Quest TV. The same z-depth system was applied to the Meta Quest TV app. The bottom navigation (Browse, Your Media, Saved) sits elevated on its own depth plane, consistent with the Store’s action bar treatment. Pressing the filter icon in the top-right triggers a Filter panel that flies onto the screen from the right — Content type options (VR Video, Video, Video Animation) sitting on their own z-layer, visually separated from the content grid behind it. The filter doesn’t overlay the content flat — it arrives with spatial presence, reinforcing that this is a temporary, interactive layer the user summoned. The content area (Top Shelf Videos, Immersive Quill Art) stays on the base plane, maintaining the same layered hierarchy across product surfaces. When a user selects a video, the player lifts forward and tilts on top of the TV panel — playback controls, progress bar, and settings all sitting on the highest z-layer while the browse surface recedes behind it. Three distinct depth planes — system bar, TV panel, video player — each communicating its role through elevation alone. Even within the player, the pattern continues: tapping the settings icon triggers a settings panel that lifts above the player controls on yet another z-layer — depth stacking that keeps each interaction contextual without navigating away from the content.

03

People App — Layered Social Interactions

The z-depth system extended into the People app, where layered depth became essential for managing multiple levels of social information on a single surface.

Instructional tooltip. A dark banner (“You can access your contact list here”) floats above the main content on its own z-layer — visually distinct from the interface below, clearly temporary, and dismissible. Depth signals that this is guidance sitting on top of the UI, not part of it.

People card popup. Tapping on a contact opens a profile card that lifts forward from the friends list — showing the person’s avatar, online status, current activity (“is in [App name]”), and action CTAs. The card’s elevation makes it feel like a focused layer pulled out of the list, not a page navigation. Clicking a CTA on the card triggers another z-depth popup with additional options — depth stacking that keeps context without losing the underlying surface.

Discover groups panel. The right side of the People app surfaces group recommendations (Hardcore Spies, Competitive, Sporting Friends) with Join / Open actions, sitting on its own depth plane alongside the friends list. Two information streams, two z-layers, both legible at a glance.

04

System Bar & Cross-Surface Depth

The z-depth system extended beyond the store panel to the system bar and other OS surfaces. The system bar sits on the lowest z-plane — always present, never competing with the active panel above it. When a panel opens, the depth relationship between the system bar and the content surface creates a natural visual hierarchy: background utility below, foreground content above.

A shared depth language. The goal was to establish z-depth as a reusable design primitive — not a one-off treatment for the store, but a systematic approach to spatial hierarchy that any surface on Horizon OS could adopt. Consistent depth rules across panels, tooltips, action bars, and navigation create a coherent spatial experience where users develop an intuitive sense of what’s interactive and what’s structural.

The first z-depth prototype in the design system.
Closing Thoughts

This exploration proved that z-depth isn’t a visual effect — it’s a structural tool. In VR, where every pixel exists in three-dimensional space, depth is the most natural way to communicate hierarchy, state, and interactivity. A lifted action bar tells your body it’s important before your eyes read the label. A tooltip that floats toward you says “I’m responding to you” without any animation or color change.

The z-depth patterns from this prototype sparked cross-team discussions about how depth should be used systematically across Horizon OS, influenced the engineering roadmap for spatial UI rendering, and were adopted into premium experiences on the platform. What started as a design system experiment became a shared language for how interfaces should feel in spatial computing.
