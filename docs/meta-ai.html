Meta AI — Day 0 Experience

Designing how AI shows up in spatial computing — from invocation to conversation, prototyped end-to-end in two weeks.

Horizon OS Unity 3D C# Spatial AI Voice Interaction Motion Design Strategic Initiative Leadership Review
Role
Design + Prototype
Timeline
2 weeks
Responsibilities
Product vision · Interaction design · Motion design · Unity prototyping · C# implementation · Storyboarding · Leadership demos
Outcome
Used as the Day 0 reference experience for leadership alignment on Meta AI in Horizon OS.
Summary

As Meta AI became a first-class experience on Horizon OS, the team needed to define how conversational AI should appear, respond, and guide users in VR — from first invocation through task completion. Rather than relying on static mockups, leadership needed an immersive prototype they could step into to evaluate the experience in its intended medium.

I led the end-to-end design and implementation of the Day 0 experience, defining the interaction model, spatial UI system, motion language, dynamic card architecture, disambiguation patterns, voice-driven workflow, and the complete storyboard used during leadership reviews. I fully implemented the prototype in Unity/C#, building reusable animation systems and composable UI components that enabled rapid iteration while laying the foundation for future interaction concepts. This case study highlights the four key design decisions that shaped the Meta AI experience on Horizon OS.

01

Dynamic Cards — Presenting AI Responses

AI responses vary widely in structure — from a simple weather forecast to a multi-step recipe or restaurant recommendation. A single layout couldn’t accommodate every response type, and presenting everything at once in VR quickly led to visual overload.

I designed a composable dynamic card system with a progressive top-to-bottom reveal that mirrors the user’s natural reading pattern. Rather than displaying all content at once, each card builds on screen — primary information appears first, secondary details follow. The reveal direction aligns with the card’s expansion motion, creating a cohesive interaction where motion consistently reinforces the same spatial logic.

Progressive disclosure reduced cognitive load while establishing a predictable reading rhythm across every response type. The directional reveal made dense content feel approachable, and the consistent motion language allowed users to process different response types without relearning the interface.

Maintaining spatial context. Summary cards needed a way to expand into detailed views, but a flat page transition would break spatial context — users would lose track of where they came from. I designed expansion animations where a summary card lifts and scales into a detail view with spatial depth, guiding the user’s gaze and signaling what’s interactive. Motion became information. The expansion animation preserved spatial context by showing users where they came from and how to return, reducing disorientation in an unfamiliar AI interface.

Balancing voice and direct manipulation. Voice is the primary interaction model for AI in VR, but it isn’t always the most efficient. Actions like saving a recommendation or selecting a restaurant are often faster through direct manipulation than spoken commands. I designed and implemented interactive CTAs directly on the card surface — buttons, action links, and selection options that provide a direct manipulation path alongside voice control. Users could fluidly switch between conversation and direct manipulation — using voice for open-ended requests and touch for fast, precise actions — without breaking the interaction flow.

Building a scalable card system. Designing bespoke layouts for every AI response wouldn’t scale as new capabilities were added. I built the cards as composable UI primitives — header, content, actions, media — that could be assembled into different layouts while maintaining a consistent interaction model. The card primitives became reusable building blocks that allowed new AI experiences to be prototyped rapidly without redesigning or rebuilding the UI from scratch.

Where does AI live in spatial space?
02

The AI Ring — Spatial Presence

The central design question: where does AI live in spatial space? A flat overlay would ignore the medium. A fully 3D agent would be distracting. The AI ring was designed as a middle ground — a persistent, spatially anchored presence that signals availability without competing with the user’s environment.

Size and position relative to the panel. The ring needed to feel connected to the main content surface without being part of it. Too close and it reads as a button; too far and the relationship breaks. I tuned the spatial relationship through rapid in-headset iteration — adjusting scale, offset, and depth until the ring felt like a companion to the panel, not an accessory.

A visual anchor for AI presence. The ring doesn’t just mark where AI lives — it signals state. Idle, listening, processing, responding — each mode has a distinct visual treatment that the user learns to read without explicit labels. The goal: make AI presence legible at a glance, even in peripheral vision.

03

Disambiguation — Designing for Ambiguity

AI doesn’t always have a clean answer. “Find me a good Italian place” — near home? Near the office? For two or for six? These moments of ambiguity can’t stall the experience. The disambiguation interface turns clarification into a structured, low-friction interaction.

Clarification as conversation, not error. The pattern frames follow-up questions as a natural part of the dialogue — “I found a few options, which did you mean?” — rather than a system failure. The spatial layout presents choices as distinct, tappable options with enough visual separation that the user can scan and decide quickly.

A reusable primitive. The disambiguation pattern wasn’t a one-off solution for the demo. It became a foundational interaction model for any moment where the AI needs more context — applicable across search, recommendations, and multi-step tasks.

Clarification should feel like conversation, not error.
04

Voice & Storyboard — The Full Journey

The prototype wasn’t a collection of screens — it was a narrative. I designed and built the complete storyboard flow that leadership would experience during review: invocation, conversation, response, follow-up, and task completion. Every transition was sequenced to tell the story of what Meta AI in VR could be. This prototype directly influenced the Meta AI user journey — shaping key milestones, defining the product direction, and establishing the interaction framework that the team built toward.

Voice as the primary input. In VR, your hands are often occupied or controllers feel heavy for text input. Voice command integration connected natural language input to the spatial system — enabling hands-free invocation (“Hey Meta”) and conversational interaction. The challenge was making voice feel responsive in a spatial context: the ring reacts immediately to speech, and the card system begins composing before the full response arrives.

A demo built to persuade. Director-level reviews don’t have time for caveats. The storyboard was paced to build confidence progressively: start with a simple query, show the card response, introduce a disambiguation moment, then land on a complex multi-step task. Each beat demonstrated a capability and a design principle. The prototype became the north-star reference for what the team was building toward.

Closing Thoughts

This project reinforced a principle I continue to believe in: the fastest path to alignment in spatial computing is a prototype people can step into. Static mockups can communicate layout, but they can’t convey depth, motion, timing, or the relationship between an AI assistant and the space around it. Building directly in Unity allowed every design decision to be experienced in context, making interaction quality something teams could feel rather than debate.

The Dynamic Cards, AI Ring, Disambiguation patterns, and Voice interactions each earned their place by solving a specific user problem — not by introducing novelty for its own sake. Questions that had stalled in slide reviews were often resolved within minutes once stakeholders experienced the prototype in headset. More than a demonstration, the prototype became a shared language for discussing the future of Meta AI in VR, aligning teams around a vision that was tangible, testable, and ready to evolve.
