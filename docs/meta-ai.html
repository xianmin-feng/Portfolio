Meta AI — Day 0 Experience

Designing how AI shows up in spatial computing — from invocation to conversation, prototyped end-to-end in two weeks.

Horizon OS Unity 3D C# Spatial AI Voice Interaction Motion Design Strategic Initiative Leadership Review
Summary

As Meta AI became a first-class surface on Horizon OS, the team needed to define what the AI journey looks and feels like in VR — from first invocation to conversation to task completion. This effort established the foundational vision for how AI presents itself in spatial computing, giving leadership something they could step into and experience rather than review on a screen.

I owned the end-to-end design and prototyping of the Day 0 experience: the complete storyboard flow, visual UI primitives, dynamic card system, disambiguation interfaces, expansion animations, and voice-driven spatial interaction. This case study walks through four design decisions that defined the Meta AI journey on Horizon OS.

01

Dynamic Cards — Presenting AI Responses

AI responses aren’t uniform — a weather answer looks nothing like a recipe or a restaurant recommendation. The dynamic card system was designed as a flexible framework where content populates modular cards that adapt their layout to the content type.

Progressive reveal animation. I designed the card content to reveal progressively from top to bottom, following the user’s natural reading pattern. Primary information appears first, secondary details follow — reinforcing the visual hierarchy through motion rather than static layout alone. The animation guides the user’s attention smoothly through the content, making each card easier to scan and understand as it builds on screen. Progressive disclosure makes dense content feel less overwhelming, and the directional reveal creates a polished, intentional motion experience. The reveal direction aligns with the card’s expansion motion, creating a cohesive interaction where every movement reinforces the same spatial logic. A top-to-bottom reveal aligns with users’ natural reading behavior, reinforces the information hierarchy, guides visual attention, reduces cognitive load, and creates a smoother, more cohesive expansion experience.

Expansion animations that communicate hierarchy. A summary card lifts and scales into a detail view with spatial depth. The transition isn’t decorative — it reinforces the relationship between overview and deep-dive, guiding the user’s gaze and signaling what’s interactive. In VR, motion is information: the expansion tells you where you came from and how to get back.

CTA as a secondary input channel. I designed and implemented interactive CTAs directly on the card surface — buttons, action links, and selection options that give users a direct manipulation path alongside voice control. Voice is the primary input for AI interaction, but not every moment calls for speech. Tapping a CTA to select a restaurant, save a recommendation, or expand a result provides a precise, low-effort alternative that complements the conversational flow without interrupting it.

Cards as a design primitive. Rather than designing individual screens for each response type, I built cards as a composable system — header, body, action row — that could be assembled differently depending on the content. This gave the prototype flexibility to demonstrate a range of AI capabilities without bespoke UI for each one.

Where does AI live in spatial space?
02

The AI Ring — Spatial Presence

The central design question: where does AI live in spatial space? A flat overlay would ignore the medium. A fully 3D agent would be distracting. The AI ring was designed as a middle ground — a persistent, spatially anchored presence that signals availability without competing with the user’s environment.

Size and position relative to the panel. The ring needed to feel connected to the main content surface without being part of it. Too close and it reads as a button; too far and the relationship breaks. I tuned the spatial relationship through rapid in-headset iteration — adjusting scale, offset, and depth until the ring felt like a companion to the panel, not an accessory.

A visual anchor for AI presence. The ring doesn’t just mark where AI lives — it signals state. Idle, listening, processing, responding — each mode has a distinct visual treatment that the user learns to read without explicit labels. The goal: make AI presence legible at a glance, even in peripheral vision.

03

Disambiguation — Designing for Ambiguity

AI doesn’t always have a clean answer. “Find me a good Italian place” — near home? Near the office? For two or for six? These moments of ambiguity can’t stall the experience. The disambiguation interface turns clarification into a structured, low-friction interaction.

Clarification as conversation, not error. The pattern frames follow-up questions as a natural part of the dialogue — “I found a few options, which did you mean?” — rather than a system failure. The spatial layout presents choices as distinct, tappable options with enough visual separation that the user can scan and decide quickly.

A reusable primitive. The disambiguation pattern wasn’t a one-off solution for the demo. It became a foundational interaction model for any moment where the AI needs more context — applicable across search, recommendations, and multi-step tasks.

Clarification should feel like conversation, not error.
04

Voice & Storyboard — The Full Journey

The prototype wasn’t a collection of screens — it was a narrative. I designed and built the complete storyboard flow that leadership would experience during review: invocation, conversation, response, follow-up, and task completion. Every transition was sequenced to tell the story of what Meta AI in VR could be.

Voice as the primary input. In VR, your hands are often occupied or controllers feel heavy for text input. Voice command integration connected natural language input to the spatial system — enabling hands-free invocation (“Hey Meta”) and conversational interaction. The challenge was making voice feel responsive in a spatial context: the ring reacts immediately to speech, and the card system begins composing before the full response arrives.

A demo built to persuade. Director-level reviews don’t have time for caveats. The storyboard was paced to build confidence progressively: start with a simple query, show the card response, introduce a disambiguation moment, then land on a complex multi-step task. Each beat demonstrated a capability and a design principle. The prototype became the north-star reference for what the team was building toward.

Closing Thoughts

This project reinforced that in spatial computing, the fastest path to alignment is a prototype you can step into. Flat mockups can’t convey depth, motion, or the spatial relationship between an AI ring and the panel it serves. Working in code meant every design decision was testable in the body, not debatable on a screen.

The ring system, dynamic cards, disambiguation patterns, and voice integration — each earned its place not by being novel, but by being honest about what it was doing for the user. Design questions that had stalled in slide reviews were resolved within minutes of in-headset walkthroughs. That’s the value of building in the medium you’re designing for.
