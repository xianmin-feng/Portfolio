Horizon OS Media Experience

Prototyping the future of media consumption in VR — from ambiguous concept to shipped product at Connect 2025.

Horizon OS Unity 3D C# AR / VR / MR Media & Entertainment Motion Design Shipped Product Connect 2025
Summary

I led prototyping for the Media Watch Experience on Horizon OS — driving both the HzTV app and an immersive cinema effort from ambiguous concept to a clear, leadership-backed product direction. The HzTV app shipped at Connect 2025 as the central media hub on the platform.

Over three months, I delivered 45 production builds, powered weekly in-headset playtesting with cross-functional leads, and built prototypes that became the engineering team’s working reference for implementation. This case study walks through three layers of that work: the interactive UX, the cinema experience, and the design-to-engineering bridge that made it ship.

01

HzTV App — Interactive UX in Working Code

The HzTV app needed to feel like a native media hub — not a ported 2D player dropped into VR. I designed and built the interactive UX entirely in working code: splash screen, landing page, content browsing, scrolling, 2.5D treatments, transitions, and animations.

Splash screen — iterating in Unity. The first thing users see sets the tone. I explored multiple splash screen animations rapidly in Unity — testing timing, easing, and sequencing in the headset. The version that landed: a full-screen HzTV logo greets the user on launch. On press, the logo shrinks and settles into the bottom-right corner as the landing page comes alive behind it — featured wallpapers begin their crossfade loop, and the app feels like it’s already breathing. The transition from branding moment to content surface happens in one continuous motion, so the user never feels like they’re waiting for the app to “load.”

Wallpaper crossfade — ambient motion. The landing page hero area cycles through featured movie and show wallpapers with a slow crossfade. The transition is deliberately unhurried — smooth enough to feel cinematic, slow enough not to compete with the user’s attention. It gives the landing page a sense of life and editorial curation without requiring any interaction, making the media hub feel like a living storefront rather than a static menu.

Parallax on content categories. Each content category row uses a parallax effect — as the user scrolls, thumbnails and labels shift at slightly different rates, creating a subtle sense of depth. In VR, this does more than look polished: it reinforces spatial hierarchy, makes browsing feel tactile, and adds a moment of delight to what would otherwise be a flat content grid. The effect was tuned in-headset until it felt natural — present enough to notice, restrained enough not to distract from the content itself.

Design through in-headset iteration. Every interaction was tested where it would live — in the headset, not on a monitor. I’d build a version, put it on, feel what worked and what didn’t, and refine. This tight loop ran weekly with cross-functional leads, turning open design questions into go/no-go decisions within minutes. 45 builds in three months — each addressing specific questions: Does this scroll feel right? Does the parallax depth add or distract? Where does the eye land first?

The prototype became the engineering team’s working reference.
02

Immersive Cinema — From Exploration to Strategy

Beyond the app, I spearheaded prototyping for an immersive cinema experience. The core challenge was twofold: define a media-watching flow that feels intriguing and seamless within the Horizon OS ecosystem, and push past technical limitations to create something that doesn’t just replicate a theater — but delivers an experience you can’t get anywhere else.

Giant Screen Resolved a team debate by building a working prototype: drag-and-drop media toward the sky, and a giant screen appears overhead.
Co-watching Invite flow anchored in the Navigator’s People tab. Solo to shared viewing with no mode switch and no new UI to learn.
Cinema World Travel into an immersive cinema with friends. Media carries over, drag-and-drop still works — same patterns, new atmosphere.
Arrakis Experience Dynamic lighting casts from the film onto the space — shadows shift with the scene, ambient light responds to on-screen action.
3P Takeover Third-party content providers take over the viewing surface, creating branded, curated media experiences.
Allocentric Bloom Transition animation that expands content from a focal point outward, guiding the user’s gaze into immersive viewing.

From exploration to clear strategy. Over five months of prototype-driven reviews with leadership, the cinema effort moved from open-ended exploration to a clear product direction — positioned to become a significant feature for the platform. My prototypes gave leadership something tangible to react to, accelerating decisions that had stalled in abstract discussion and establishing a strong foundation for future innovations.

03

Bridging Design & Engineering

Prototypes that stop at proof of concept create a handoff gap. Mine didn’t. Because the prototypes were built in real, running code, they became the engineering team’s working reference — directly shaping how features were implemented.

Parallax adopted into production. The parallax effect I designed for the content category rows was directly referenced and adopted by the engineering team into the shipped product. What started as a design exploration in Unity became a production interaction pattern — proof that high-fidelity prototyping doesn’t just inform engineering decisions, it sets the standard.

Interaction details, resolved before development. Engineers could open the prototype, inspect the behavior, and align on interaction details and technical approach before writing production code. Animation timing, scroll physics, transition curves, spatial depth — these were validated in the prototype, not negotiated during implementation. That cut rework significantly.

Design judgment and engineering craft, reinforcing each other. The prototype wasn’t a spec thrown over the wall. It was a shared artifact where design intent and engineering reality met. When an animation felt right in the headset but was too expensive to render, we knew immediately — and iterated on the trade-off together, in code.

Design judgment and engineering craft, reinforcing each other.
Closing Thoughts

The HzTV app shipped at Connect 2025 with hundreds of titles, establishing “TV” as the central media hub on Horizon OS. The parallax interactions I prototyped were adopted directly into the production build. The cinema explorations — from Giant Screen to Arrakis — laid the foundation for the next generation of immersive media on the platform. This work didn’t just address technical limitations; it defined what a compelling media-watching experience looks and feels like in VR.

Over dozens of builds, the prototypes evolved alongside the product itself. Some ideas shipped directly, others informed future explorations, and many changed through repeated testing with leadership and engineering. Building in Unity meant every discussion happened around experiences people could actually use, making trade-offs easier to evaluate and product direction faster to align on.

Looking back, the project wasn’t simply about designing a media application. It was about exploring what media consumption could become in spatial computing — and using prototypes to turn uncertain ideas into concrete product decisions.
