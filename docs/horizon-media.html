Horizon OS Media Experience

A 0→1 product: designing what watching media should feel like in VR — from an ambiguous concept to a shipped media hub at Connect 2025.

0→1 Product Design UX Design Interaction Design Motion Design Unity Prototyping AR / VR Shipped at Connect 2025
Role
Design + Prototyping Lead · 0→1
Timeline
Mar – Sep 2025 · Kickoff → 2 leadership review rounds → Launch at Connect
Responsibilities
UX design · Interaction design · Motion design · Product direction · In-headset playtesting · Unity/C# prototyping · Engineering handoff
Outcome
Launched at Connect 2025 as the central media hub on Horizon OS; prototype patterns shipped nearly 1:1; carried forward to Meta’s newest headset in 2026.
Team
Design: Me (UX, interaction & motion design, prototyping) · Content designer · Art director + art designer (Cinema World 3D assets)
Leadership: 2 design managers (Horizon OS System, Media) · My manager · Design director · Consumer PM director

Summary

Situation. Watching media is one of the most common things people do in a headset — yet the default was a 2D player dropped into VR, ignoring everything that makes the medium special: depth, scale, presence, and other people. This was 0→1: no existing media hub or cinema experience to iterate on, and no shared vision — discussions kept stalling in the abstract.

Task. As design and prototyping lead for the Media Watch Experience on Horizon OS, I needed to define what watching media should feel like — the HzTV app as the platform’s media hub, plus an immersive cinema experience — and align leadership on a direction that could ship at Connect 2025.

Action. I owned the experience end to end: the structure of the hub, the interaction model for browsing and watching, the motion language, and how depth communicates hierarchy in spatial UI. I set four design principles to guide every call, and designed in the medium: 45 playable builds, weekly in-headset reviews with cross-functional leads, and two rounds of leadership reviews.

Result. HzTV launched at Connect 2025 as the central media hub on Horizon OS, and nearly every pattern from the prototype shipped as designed — parallax, navigation and page-transition motion, title layouts, and scrolling. A year later, it carried forward to Meta’s newest headset.

Learning. In a new medium, the fastest way to align a room is to let people feel the decision. Prototyping turned abstract debates into concrete choices — and kept design intent intact all the way to production.

01

The Problem — A Flat Player in a Spatial World

The design question

How might we make media on Horizon OS feel native to the medium — a living hub people want to browse, and a viewing experience you can’t get anywhere else — while staying simple, familiar, and within real platform performance limits?

Design Principles

01

Native, not ported

Use depth and space to communicate hierarchy — don’t just float a flat screen in front of the user.

02

Motion with restraint

Motion should add life and orientation, never compete with the content. If it’s noticed before the movie, it’s too much.

03

One pattern, every context

Learn an interaction once and it works everywhere — in the app, with friends, in a shared cinema.

04

Decide in the headset

Spatial design can’t be judged on a monitor. Every decision is validated where it will actually live.

02

HzTV App — Designing a Hub That Feels Alive

The HzTV app needed to be the front door to media on Horizon OS. I owned the interaction and motion design end-to-end — splash, landing page, content browsing, scrolling, 2.5D treatments, and transitions. Three decisions defined how it feels.

Decision 1 — The splash screen should never feel like loading. A static logo followed by a cut to the landing page reads as a wait. I explored multiple splash animations in Unity, testing timing, easing, and sequencing in-headset. The version that landed: a full-screen HzTV logo that, on press, shrinks and settles into the bottom-right corner while the landing page comes alive behind it. Why: the brand moment and the content surface become one continuous motion, so the user is already browsing before they notice the transition — and the logo stays on screen as a persistent anchor.

Decision 2 — Ambient motion on the landing page, deliberately slow. The hero area cycles through featured movie and show wallpapers with a crossfade. The key design call was pacing: faster felt like an ad carousel competing for attention; slower felt cinematic. Trade-off: I chose a deliberately unhurried crossfade — the landing page gets a sense of life and editorial curation without asking anything of the user, turning a static menu into a living storefront.

Decision 3 — Parallax to communicate hierarchy, not decoration. A flat content grid in VR feels like a website in a box. Each category row uses parallax — thumbnails and labels shift at slightly different rates as the user scrolls — creating a subtle sense of depth. Why: in a spatial interface, depth is information — it reinforces what’s in front and what’s behind, and makes browsing feel tactile. Trade-off: too much depth pulls focus from the content and adds visual strain, so I tuned it in-headset until it was present enough to notice but restrained enough to disappear behind the thumbnails.

How decisions got made. Each build was scoped to answer a specific question — Does this scroll feel right? Does the parallax depth add or distract? Where does the eye land first? — and weekly in-headset playtests turned those questions into go/no-go calls within minutes.

In a spatial interface, depth isn’t decoration — it’s information.
03

Exploration — Getting HzTV One Tap Away

A media hub only works if people can get to it quickly. Watching is a high-frequency activity, so I explored how HzTV should be launched from the system — not just how it behaves once it’s open.

1The goal

Make HzTV launchable in one tap from the Horizon OS Navigator, so watching something is never more than a step away.

2Ideal vs. shipped
Quick Access in NavigatorExplored

A pinned HzTV shortcut alongside other system shortcuts — explored in partnership with the Library team, who own Quick Access.

App LibraryShipped

HzTV launched through its configuration in the App Library — reachable, but one step further away than the ideal.

3The constraint

Quick Access is shared system real estate with its own owners and priorities. We couldn’t reach alignment across teams on adding HzTV there.

What I learned: entry points are a cross-team product decision, not just a design one. Designing the app well isn’t enough if the way in belongs to someone else. Next time, I’d bring the owning team in earlier — with the prototype in hand — and align on a shared goal before proposing a specific placement.

04

Immersive Cinema — Familiar Patterns, New Atmosphere

Beyond the app, I drove UX, interaction, and motion design and prototyping for an immersive cinema experience — partnering with an art director and art designer who created the 3D environment assets. The goal was twofold: define a media-watching flow that feels seamless within the Horizon OS ecosystem, and go beyond replicating a theater — to deliver something only VR can offer.

Core design decision · One interaction, many contexts

Rather than inventing a new UI for every viewing mode, I anchored the whole experience on a single gesture users already knew — drag-and-drop media. Travel into Cinema World with friends and the media carries over; drag-and-drop still works. Invite friends from the Navigator’s People tab, and solo viewing becomes shared viewing with no mode switch. Why: each new capability adds atmosphere, not cognitive load.

  • 01 Cinema World A dreamy, immersive cinema you travel into with friends to watch together.
  • 02 Co-watching Shared viewing started from the People tab users already know — no new surface to learn.
  • 03 Arrakis Experience The film extends into the room — spotlights driven by the screen cast light and reflections onto the floor as the scene changes.
  • 04 Allocentric Bloom Content expands outward from a focal point, guiding the user’s gaze from browsing into immersive viewing.
  • 05 3P Takeover Third-party providers brand and curate the viewing surface while the core interaction model stays intact.
  • 06 Giant Screen Cut Prototyped two entry points for a contested concept; cut after in-headset testing with leadership — see the deep dive.

Decision Deep Dive — Should Giant Screen Exist?

1The disagreement

The HzTV window can already be resized by dragging its corners — scaled up, it effectively is a giant screen. I argued Giant Screen duplicated existing functionality and should be cut. Leads and managers still wanted to validate the concept before deciding.

2Options tested
Baseline — Resize HzTVKept

Drag the window’s corners to make it as big as you want. Already existed.

A — Drag to the skyCut

Drag-and-drop a title onto a placeholder in the sky to watch on a giant screen overhead.

B — Cast from control barCut

Cast directly from the control bar to the giant screen.

3Validation

Leads and director-level stakeholders tried both versions in-headset. Neither entry point made Giant Screen feel meaningfully different from simply enlarging the HzTV window.

4Decision & trade-off

We cut Giant Screen. We gave up a visually impressive moment — in exchange for one clear way to make content bigger, less to learn, and timeline focused on the features that mattered.

My takeaway: when I disagree, I don’t just argue — I build the fastest experience that lets the room decide. The goal isn’t to be right; it’s for the team to reach the decision with evidence instead of opinion.

Decision Deep Dive — Where Should Cinema World Live?

1The question

Cinema World needed an entry point, and the team was split on where it belonged: inside the media app, or at the system level alongside other virtual spaces.

2Options tested
A — Inside HzTVChosen

An entry inside the HzTV app — tap in and travel to a dreamy cinema world to watch.

B — Navigator SpacesNot chosen

A set of cinema worlds under the Spaces tab in the Horizon OS Navigator.

3Why HzTV won
  • Intent. People arrive wanting to watch something, so the entry belongs next to the content — not in a list of places.
  • Order. From Spaces, users pick a world first, then have to find something to watch. From HzTV, content comes first and carries into the world.
  • Framing. Inside HzTV it reads as an upgraded way to watch; in Spaces it was just one more environment competing for attention.
4What we gave up

System-level visibility. A Navigator entry would reach people who never open HzTV. We traded that reach for a flow that matches how people actually decide to watch.

Where it landed: HzTV became the recommended entry point, but Cinema World was deprioritized for the Connect 2025 timeline and didn’t ship. The exploration still did its job — it settled where immersive viewing belongs in the platform, so the next team doesn’t start from zero.

05

Keeping Design Intent Intact to Production

Design quality is often lost in handoff — timing gets approximated, curves get simplified, depth gets flattened. I prototyped in real, running code so the design itself became the spec.

The prototype became the reference. Engineers could open the prototype, inspect the behavior, and align on interaction details before writing production code. Animation timing, scroll physics, transition curves, and spatial depth were validated in the prototype, not negotiated during implementation. Because engineers ran the same build I designed in, feasibility questions surfaced early and we resolved them together, in code — reducing rework.

Outcome
  • Launched at Connect 2025. HzTV shipped with hundreds of titles, establishing “TV” as the central media hub on Horizon OS.
  • Built to last. A year after launch, HzTV carried forward to Meta’s newest headset, announced at Connect 2026 — the design foundation holding up on new hardware.
  • Prototype to production, nearly 1:1. The parallax pattern, navigation and page-transition motion, title layouts, and vertical and horizontal scrolling all shipped as designed in the prototype.
  • Foundation for what’s next. The cinema explorations — from Cinema World to Arrakis — left the team with a validated, prototyped direction for future immersive media — even though Cinema World itself didn’t ship for Connect 2025.
