Horizon OS Media Experience

A 0→1 product: designing what watching media should feel like in VR — from an ambiguous concept to a shipped media hub at Connect 2025.

Summary

Situation. Meta wanted Quest to be a place for lean-back entertainment, not just games — to reach a broader, less gaming-focused audience. But watching on Quest meant opening separate streaming apps, each a flat 2D panel that ignored what makes VR special: depth, scale, presence, and other people. HzTV had to become the front door to all of it — redesigned from the ground up, with no shared vision of what “TV in a headset” should feel like, and discussions stalling in the abstract.

Task. As design and prototyping lead for the Media Watch Experience on Horizon OS, I owned two parallel workstreams. (1) HzTV — design and build the platform’s new entertainment hub, committed to ship at Connect 2025. (2) Cinema World — explore immersive viewing beyond the hub: traveling from your MR or VR home into a dreamy theater world to watch, with screen-driven and ambient lighting that extends the film into the space. Unlike HzTV, its path to launch was open — feasibility and engineering cost still had to be proven. Across both, I needed to align leadership on a clear direction.

Action. I owned the experience end to end: the structure of the hub, the interaction model for browsing and watching, the motion language, and how depth communicates hierarchy in spatial UI. I designed in the medium: 45 playable builds, weekly in-headset reviews with cross-functional leads, and two rounds of leadership reviews.

Result. HzTV launched at Connect 2025 as the central media hub on Horizon OS, and nearly every pattern from the prototype shipped as designed — parallax, navigation and page-transition motion, title layouts, and scrolling.

Learning. In a new medium, the fastest way to align a room is to let people feel the decision. Prototyping turned abstract debates into concrete choices — and kept design intent intact all the way to production.

01

The Problem — Great Content, Scattered Across Apps

Movies, shows, and sports were spread across separate streaming apps, like YouTube and Prime Video. To find something to watch, people had to open one app, browse, close it, and try the next. There was no single place to discover what to watch.

HzTV’s job was to bring titles from partners like Disney+, Hulu, ESPN, and Universal Pictures into one place — so people browse content, not apps.

The design question

How might we bring movies, shows, and sports from many partners into one place people want to browse — familiar enough for anyone, and native to VR?

“Horizon TV … does look a lot like your typical smart TV screen. That’s no accident: ‘We absolutely have taken inspiration from the navigation systems that have come before,’ says Meta Reality Labs director of entertainment content Sarah Malkin. ‘We want customers to feel a sense of familiarity.’”Janko Roettgers, Lowpass, Oct 2025

Design Principles

01

Native, not ported

Use depth and space to communicate hierarchy — don’t just float a flat screen in front of the user.

02

Motion with restraint

Motion should add life and orientation, never compete with the content. If it’s noticed before the movie, it’s too much.

03

Familiar by design

Navigation people already know from traditional TV — a hero banner, category tabs, rows of titles — so even first-time VR users can start exploring the moment they put on the headset.

04

Decide in the headset

Spatial design can’t be judged on a monitor. Every decision is validated where it will actually live.

02

HzTV App — Designing a Hub That Feels Alive

The HzTV app needed to be the front door to media on Horizon OS. I owned the interaction and motion design end-to-end — splash, landing page, content browsing, scrolling, 2.5D treatments, and transitions. Three decisions defined how it feels.

Decision 1

The splash screen should never feel like loading.

A static logo followed by a cut to the landing page reads as a wait. I explored multiple splash animations in Unity, testing timing, easing, and sequencing in-headset. The version that landed: a full-screen HzTV logo that, on press, shrinks and settles into the bottom-right corner while the landing page comes alive behind it. Why: the brand moment and the content surface become one continuous motion, so the user is already browsing before they notice the transition — and the logo stays on screen as a persistent anchor.

Decision 2

Ambient motion on the landing page, deliberately slow.

The hero area cycles through featured movie and show wallpapers with a crossfade. The key design call was pacing: faster felt like an ad carousel competing for attention; slower felt cinematic. Trade-off: I chose a deliberately unhurried crossfade — the landing page gets a sense of life and editorial curation without asking anything of the user, turning a static menu into a living storefront.

Decision 3

Parallax to communicate hierarchy, not decoration.

A flat content grid in VR feels like a website in a box. Each category row uses parallax — thumbnails and labels shift at slightly different rates as the user scrolls — creating a subtle sense of depth. Why: in a spatial interface, depth is information — it reinforces what’s in front and what’s behind, and makes browsing feel tactile. Trade-off: too much depth pulls focus from the content and adds visual strain, so I tuned it in-headset until it was present enough to notice but restrained enough to disappear behind the thumbnails.

How decisions got made. Each build was scoped to answer a specific question — Does this scroll feel right? Does the parallax depth add or distract? Where does the eye land first? — and weekly in-headset playtests turned those questions into go/no-go calls within minutes.

Exploration — Switching Without Leaving the Screen

HzTV is a hub: picking a title hands off to the partner app — House of the Dragon plays in Max, Mission: Impossible in Prime Video. Once something was playing, switching to another title meant going back to the HzTV home page and browsing all over again.

1The friction

Changing what you watch meant leaving the screen you were on and starting over from the HzTV home page.

2Options
A — Allocentric BloomExplored

A concept I initiated: while media plays, the HzTV home shrinks into a small menu below the panel. Switch titles or jump back home straight from that menu — no round trip through a back button — so people stay focused on what they’re watching.

B — Back arrowShipped

A back arrow on the HzTV panel that returns from the playing title to the HzTV home page in one step, so moving between titles feels fluent.

3Where it landed

I reviewed Bloom with engineering. Switching content in place across partner apps needed significant effort, time, and people — so we shipped the back arrow: simpler to build, at the cost of an extra trip home every time you switch.

What I learned: the idea was strong, but I designed it at the feature level — a solution that fit this one case. Switching content in place touches system-level surfaces owned by other teams. Next time I’d bring in the panel management team early and work through the system-level questions first, so the design fits the platform, not just one project.

Exploration — Where HzTV Lives in the System

Reaching non-gaming users is a core bet in Meta’s entertainment push, so getting people into HzTV quickly mattered as much as the app itself. I explored where HzTV should live in the Horizon OS app library:

Quick Access rowShipped

The first row people see when they open the app library — HzTV one tap away.

App Library gridShipped

Alongside every other app, for broader visibility and discoverability.

We shipped both: Quick Access for speed, and the full grid for visibility — two paths into the same hub.

In a spatial interface, depth isn’t decoration — it’s information.
03

Cinema World — Familiar Patterns, New Atmosphere

Beyond the app, I drove UX, interaction, and motion design and prototyping for an immersive cinema experience — partnering with an art director and art designer who created the 3D environment assets. The goal was twofold: define a media-watching flow that feels seamless within the Horizon OS ecosystem, and go beyond replicating a theater — to deliver something only VR can offer.

Core design work · One end-to-end flow

I designed and prototyped the full social viewing journey end to end, so the team could test the whole experience instead of isolated screens: start watching solo, invite friends from the Navigator’s People tab, and move into shared viewing with no mode switch; travel together into Cinema World with the media carrying over; and handle the realities of streaming rights — like each guest signing in to the streaming service (DRM) before everyone can watch together.

  • 01 Cinema World A dreamy, immersive cinema you travel into with friends to watch together.
  • 02 Co-watching Shared viewing started from the People tab users already know — no new surface to learn.
  • 03 Arrakis Experience A Dune-themed viewing environment for HzTV. The film extends into the room: spotlights follow the color and brightness of each scene, casting light and reflections across the floor, so the space feels like part of the film.
  • 04 Ambient Light While media plays, ambient light is generated from what’s on screen and spills into the surrounding space — a more immersive, delightful way to watch.
  • 05 Giant Screen Cut Prototyped two entry points for a contested concept; cut after in-headset testing with leadership — see the deep dive.

Decision Deep Dive — Should Giant Screen Exist?

1The disagreement

The HzTV window can already be resized by dragging its corners — scaled up, it effectively is a giant screen. I argued Giant Screen duplicated existing functionality and should be cut. Leads and managers still wanted to validate the concept before deciding.

2Options tested
Baseline — Resize HzTVKept

Drag the window’s corners to make it as big as you want. Already existed.

A — Drag to the skyCut

Drag-and-drop a title onto a placeholder in the sky to watch on a giant screen overhead.

B — Cast from control barCut

Cast directly from the control bar to the giant screen.

3Validation

Leads and director-level stakeholders tried both versions in-headset. Neither entry point made Giant Screen feel meaningfully different from simply enlarging the HzTV window.

4Decision & trade-off

We cut Giant Screen. We gave up a visually impressive moment — in exchange for one clear way to make content bigger, less to learn, and timeline focused on the features that mattered.

My takeaway: when I disagree, I don’t just argue — I build the fastest experience that lets the room decide. The goal isn’t to be right; it’s for the team to reach the decision with evidence instead of opinion.

Decision Deep Dive — Where Should Cinema World Live?

1The question

Cinema World needed an entry point, and the team was split on where it belonged: inside the media app, or at the system level alongside other virtual spaces.

2Options tested
A — Inside HzTVChosen

An entry inside the HzTV app — tap in and travel to a dreamy cinema world to watch.

B — Navigator SpacesNot chosen

A set of cinema worlds under the Spaces tab in the Horizon OS Navigator.

3Why HzTV won
  • Intent. People arrive wanting to watch something, so the entry belongs next to the content — not in a list of places.
  • Order. From Spaces, users pick a world first, then have to find something to watch. From HzTV, content comes first and carries into the world.
  • Framing. Inside HzTV it reads as an upgraded way to watch; in Spaces it was just one more environment competing for attention.
4What we gave up

System-level visibility. A Navigator entry would reach people who never open HzTV. We traded that reach for a flow that matches how people actually decide to watch.

Where it landed: HzTV became the recommended entry point. The exploration settled where immersive viewing belongs in the platform, so the next team doesn’t start from zero.

04

Keeping Design Intent Intact to Production

Design quality is often lost in handoff — timing gets approximated, curves get simplified, depth gets flattened. I prototyped in real, running code so the design itself became the spec.

The prototype became the reference. Engineers could open the prototype, inspect the behavior, and align on interaction details before writing production code. Animation timing, scroll physics, transition curves, and spatial depth were validated in the prototype, not negotiated during implementation. Because engineers ran the same build I designed in, feasibility questions surfaced early and we resolved them together, in code — reducing rework.

Outcome
  • Launched at Connect 2025. HzTV debuted as Horizon OS’s new entertainment hub — movies, TV, live sports, and music in one place, with partners including Disney+, Hulu, ESPN, Prime Video, Peacock, and Universal Pictures. With Dolby Atmos spatial sound at launch and Dolby Vision announced, it’s built to feel more immersive than a traditional TV.
  • Prototype to production, nearly 1:1. The parallax pattern, navigation and page-transition motion, title layouts, and vertical and horizontal scrolling all shipped as designed in the prototype.
  • Foundation for what’s next. The cinema explorations — from Cinema World to Arrakis — left the team with a validated, prototyped direction for future immersive media.
