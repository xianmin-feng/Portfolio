Navigator Origins — Shaping Spatial OS Navigation

Implementing two Navigator variants to define how users orient, discover, and move through Horizon OS.

Horizon OS Unity 3D C# Information Architecture Spatial Navigation Z-Depth Motion Design Exploration
Summary

Before the Navigator became the surface millions of users see every time they put on a headset, the team needed to answer a fundamental question: how should content, apps, spaces, and people be organized in a spatial OS? The challenge was information architecture — defining the right hierarchy, grouping, and navigation patterns so users can find what they need without thinking about structure. I implemented two fully functional Navigator variants — Discovery and Entertainment — each proposing a different IA model for how the platform’s content should be laid out, categorized, and accessed.

Discovery tested a five-tab IA with deep sub-navigation and category-based filtering — optimized for breadth and exploration. Entertainment tested a three-tab IA with a media-first hierarchy and persistent search — optimized for speed and focus. These weren’t wireframes or slide decks. They were working prototypes in Unity that leadership could put on a headset and feel how each IA model shaped the experience. The explorations defined the navigation language that Horizon OS was built on, and the z-depth tab transition I introduced became a widely adopted pattern across the platform.

01

Discovery Variant — Designed for Finding

The Discovery variant was built around one principle: make everything easy to find in a way that matches users’ mental models. The information architecture organized the OS into five clear tabs — Explore, Spaces, People, Apps, and Search — each with its own content structure and sub-navigation.

Explore tab — a three-column layout. The default landing surface was organized as three simultaneous information streams. The left column surfaced “Your recent apps” as a compact grid with overflow indicators (+6, +7), and “Happening now” showing live events and active spaces — giving users instant context about what’s available right now. The center column held the Horizon Feed, a scrollable social content surface for posts and shared media. The right column was the Connections panel — showing the active user with online status, a Group Invite action, and a friends list with presence indicators so users could see who’s online and what they’re doing. Three columns, three types of information — recent activity, content, and people — all visible without a single tap.

Make everything easy to find in a way that matches how people already think.
02

Discovery — The Full Flow

Spaces tab. A category-based side navigation let users filter by Happening Now, Recent, and Favorites — three lenses that cover the most common reasons someone opens the Spaces tab. Users could switch between them to explore different types of spaces without losing context.

People tab. I used a map metaphor to visualize the metaverse — showing where friends are, what they’re doing, who’s online. The sub-navigation offered Found, Who is Online, Recents, Followers, and Following, giving users multiple entry points into their social graph.

Apps tab. A right-side navigation separated 2D Apps, 3D Apps, and Environments, letting users switch between content types to find what they’re interested in. The structure made the breadth of the app ecosystem scannable without overwhelming the user.

Z-depth tab transitions. When switching between tabs, the content doesn’t just swap — it moves in z-space. Each page transitions with depth-based animation, reinforcing the spatial nature of the OS and giving users a physical sense of moving between contexts. This z-depth motion language had a significant impact on the broader Horizon OS system — the pattern was adopted by designers and prototypers across the team, recognized by leadership, and shaped into later, higher-fidelity versions of the Navigator.

03

Entertainment Variant — Designed for Consumption

Where the Discovery variant optimized for breadth and exploration, the Entertainment variant optimized for focus. The layout was divided into three clear zones: top navigation, content area, and bottom main navigation — a mental model that’s immediately familiar and keeps users oriented on what they came to do: watch, play, and explore.

Media-first homepage. The default landing surface was designed like a streaming service adapted for spatial computing. A “Suggested Show” hero card dominated the top half, giving featured content prime real estate. Below it, a row of app shortcuts — YouTube, Reels, Horizon Worlds, Netflix, Xbox — provided one-tap access to the most-used entertainment destinations. Content up front, apps within reach, minimal chrome.

Three-tab structure. The bottom navigation offered Spaces, Explore, and Apps — fewer tabs, fewer decisions. Each tab had its own contextual search bar that adapted its label to the current surface: “Explore” on the homepage, “Search Spaces” in the Spaces tab, and “Search Library” in the Apps tab. One persistent pattern, but always relevant to where the user is.

Fewer tabs, fewer decisions — optimized for doing, not browsing.
04

Entertainment — The Full Flow

Top bar. People, Time, Settings, and Display controls lived in the top navigation — persistent utilities that stay out of the way until needed. The top bar kept the main content area uncluttered, reinforcing the entertainment-first hierarchy.

Apps with folders. The Apps tab introduced a folder concept — games grouped together, media grouped together. Instead of a flat list, related apps were clustered by category, making the library scannable and giving users an organizational model they already understand from mobile.

Spaces tab. Direct access to MR Home, immersive games like Beat Saber, and Horizon Home. The Spaces tab treated spatial environments as first-class destinations, not features buried inside other surfaces. The MR Home entry used a 3D spatial card with an avatar illustration, visually distinguishing it from flat app icons and signaling that this is a place you enter, not an app you open.

Pagination dots that become tabs. The bottom navigation starts as minimal pagination dots — three small circles that indicate which tab you’re on. On hover, the active dot smoothly expands into a labeled tab with an icon, revealing its identity only when the user needs it. This micro-interaction keeps the nav ultra-minimal at rest while remaining fully discoverable on intent — a small detail that solved the tension between clean chrome and clear wayfinding.

Smooth tab transitions. Switching between Spaces, Explore, and Apps triggers a fluid crossfade with subtle spatial motion. The content doesn’t just swap — it eases in with direction, giving the user a sense of moving between contexts rather than jumping between static screens. Combined with the contextual search bar and expanding dots, the transitions make three very different surfaces feel like one cohesive system.

Closing Thoughts

Two variants, two philosophies. Discovery asked: how do we help users find everything the platform offers? Entertainment asked: how do we get users to the thing they want as fast as possible? Both were valid — and both needed to be built and experienced in-headset before the team could make an informed decision about the Navigator’s direction.

The z-depth tab transition, the side-navigation patterns, and the information architecture decisions from these explorations carried forward into the Navigator that shipped. This is the work that happens before a product has a name — the foundational choices that determine whether the experience feels intuitive or arbitrary once millions of people put on the headset.
