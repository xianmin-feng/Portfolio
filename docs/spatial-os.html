Spatial OS Navigator & System Bar

Defining how navigation feels across Horizon OS — from early interaction explorations to shipped system experiences.

Horizon OS Navigator System Bar Unity 3D C# Motion Design Exploration AR / VR / MR Leadership Review Shipped Product
Summary

As the design engineer responsible for the Navigator on Horizon OS — one of the operating system’s most frequently used surfaces — I led the interaction design and Unity implementation of its motion system, visual language, and core behaviors. Working directly in code, I explored dozens of in-headset prototypes that informed product direction, executive reviews, and engineering implementation.

This case study walks through four moments along that evolution: an early exploration of how the Navigator should feel, the Aura demo that became the next-generation Horizon OS visual language, an iteration on hover-and-cast parallax for the People tab, and a contextual exit pattern for getting users back from immersive apps.

01

Navigator Homepage — Motion Exploration

Early on, the question wasn't “what should the Navigator look like” — it was “how should it feel?” This is the exploration of motion language: timing, easing, focus transitions, and the cushion between states. Working in code meant every variation could be put on a headset and judged in the body, not on a screen.

The exploration focused on the Navigator homepage, with the Meta AI tab as the focal point. The goal: simplify the Navigator and system bar while making them feel more alive and visually engaging. I introduced a dynamic motion language to evolve the Horizon OS homepage past static layout — toward something that reacts, anticipates, and breathes.

A few specific design moves I was testing:

Progressive disclosure from top to bottom. The Meta AI surface doesn't appear all at once — it reveals from the top, letting the user's eye follow the structure as it builds. The reveal itself becomes part of the welcome, instead of dumping a full panel into view.

A loading state that signals, not stalls. Before the real content arrives, a soft blue placeholder fills the card frame — a skeleton that says “something is coming” rather than leaving the user staring at empty space. Loading becomes a deliberate moment in the experience, not a gap in it.

Tab switching that respects state. The system bar's adjacent tabs — Library and People — each carry their own behaviors. When the user switches away from Meta AI, the AI pauses — conceptually as much as visually. An inactive tab shouldn't keep working in the background; the motion language reinforces it.

The underlying thinking: the homepage isn't a static layout to navigate around. It's a system with intent, where each surface anticipates, responds, and paces itself to the user's attention.

How should it feel?
02

Aura Demo

The Aura demo introduced a new visual design language for the next generation of Horizon OS — tested end-to-end across the Navigator, the system bar, and the multi-panel window surfaces.

A unified visual language across surfaces. A frosted-glass treatment runs through every panel — Navigator, Quick Controls, Library, Search — built on a shared shader system so consistency is baked into the rendering pipeline rather than enforced by convention. The result reads as one system, not a collection of screens.

A three-layer motion model on the Navigator. The home surface breaks into three depth layers: a solid color base, the icon layer that pops forward, and a cast shadow that grounds the icon back onto the base. I tweaked the timing, distance, and depth of all three relationships until the motion felt right in the body, not just on paper — landing the version that earned leadership sign-off.

Stacking for multi-panel use. Open Store, Chats, Browser, TV — and instead of scattering across space, the panels stack on top of each other. Multi-panel work becomes spatially compact, organized, and easy to recover from.

On/off transitions that breathe. When the Navigator surfaces, the world behind it blurs softly; when it dismisses, the blur fades back in step. The transition maintains continuity between immersive and mixed reality, preserving context instead of creating an abrupt mode switch.

The demo went through multiple rounds of executive and leadership review and was accepted as the visual direction for the next generation of Horizon OS.

03

Navigator People Tab — Motion Iteration

As the Navigator evolved, I focused on one question: how can motion communicate interactivity without adding visual noise? Rather than introducing new UI elements, I explored subtle depth cues that make the interface feel tangible in VR.

Motion creates spatial hierarchy. When a user hovers over a people tile, the portrait lifts forward while its circular background remains anchored in place. A soft shadow forms beneath the portrait, creating a small but meaningful parallax effect. The interaction is intentionally restrained — the combination of elevation, shadow, and timing gives users a clear sense of focus without distracting from the content. In spatial interfaces, depth isn’t decoration. It’s one of the primary ways users understand what can be interacted with.

From interaction to system. Once the hover behavior felt natural in headset, the same motion pattern was applied across other interactive surfaces, including the Apps tab. Rather than designing separate animations for each destination, I established a reusable interaction pattern that gave the Navigator a consistent motion language.

Simplifying the persistent surface. This prototype also captures an earlier version of the system bar, which contained six persistent destinations. Through multiple rounds of iteration, we reduced it to three primary entry points, improving visual clarity and lowering cognitive load on one of the operating system’s most frequently used surfaces.

Every persistent surface taxes attention.
04

Navigator System Bar — Quit from Immersive App

Designing the exit. The video walks through the transition between MR and immersive environments — specifically, how a user finds their way back when they're inside an immersive app.

A contextual button in the persistent surface. When the user enters an immersive app — Beat Saber, for example — a new CTA appears on the right side of the system bar. The bar is the OS's most persistent surface, which makes it the right place to host the exit: always reachable, never hidden in a menu the user has to remember how to summon.

Hover to preview the action. Approaching the button doesn't immediately quit. On hover, it expands to show what it will close — “Close Beat Saber” — letting the user confirm intent before committing. In immersive contexts, accidental quits are expensive: you lose state, lose immersion, and have to rebuild both.

Quitting an immersive app isn't a UI action — it's a transition between two realities. This was one of several explorations of that handoff: the CTA stays visible the whole time, previews itself before activation, and routes the user predictably back to the MR home. The exit, treated as design rather than as a click target.

Closing Thoughts

What I take from this work: the design that ships isn't the cleverest move I tried — it's the one that survived weekly playtests and still felt right in the body. Owning the Navigator meant designing across disciplines at once: motion, visual language, information architecture, interaction patterns. The real work was making those choices cohere into something that reads as one system, not a collection of decisions.

Three-layer parallax, frosted-glass continuity, hover-to-preview restraint, six system-bar buttons reduced to three — each pattern earned its place not by being novel, but by being honest about what it was doing for the user. Working in code instead of mockups is what made that honesty testable.
