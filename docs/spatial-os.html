Spatial OS Navigation & System Bar

Owning the Navigator on Horizon OS — design exploration to shipped feature, told through four moments of motion prototyping.

Horizon OS Navigator Unity 3D C# VR / MR Shipped Product

As the owner of the Navigator on Horizon OS, I drove its design and prototyping from open exploration to the version users now touch every time they put on a headset. Below: four moments along that evolution — how motion language was found, refined, and finally integrated into the broader OS.

1. Navigator Homepage — Motion Exploration

Early on, the question wasn't “what should the Navigator look like” — it was “how should it feel?” This is the exploration of motion language: timing, easing, focus transitions, and the cushion between states. Working in code meant every variation could be put on a headset and judged in the body, not on a screen.

The exploration focused on the Navigator homepage, with the Meta AI tab as the focal point. The goal: simplify the Navigator and system bar while making them feel more alive and visually engaging. I introduced a dynamic motion language to evolve the Horizon OS homepage past static layout — toward something that reacts, anticipates, and breathes.

A few specific design moves I was testing:

Progressive disclosure from top to bottom. The Meta AI surface doesn't appear all at once — it reveals from the top, letting the user's eye follow the structure as it builds. The reveal itself becomes part of the welcome, instead of dumping a full panel into view.

A loading state that signals, not stalls. Before the real content arrives, a soft blue placeholder fills the card frame — a skeleton that says “something is coming” rather than leaving the user staring at empty space. Loading becomes a deliberate moment in the experience, not a gap in it.

Tab switching that respects state. The system bar's adjacent tabs — Library and People — each carry their own behaviors. When the user switches away from Meta AI, the AI pauses — conceptually as much as visually. An inactive tab shouldn't keep working in the background; the motion language reinforces it.

The underlying thinking: the homepage isn't a static layout to navigate around. It's a system with intent, where each surface anticipates, responds, and paces itself to the user's attention.

2. Aura Demo

The Aura demo introduced a new visual design language for the next generation of Horizon OS — tested end-to-end across the Navigator, the system bar, and the multi-panel window surfaces.

A unified visual language across surfaces. A frosted-glass treatment runs through every panel — Navigator, Quick Controls, Library, Search — built on a shared shader system so consistency is baked into the rendering pipeline rather than enforced by convention. The result reads as one system, not a collection of screens.

A three-layer motion model on the Navigator. The home surface breaks into three depth layers: a solid color base, the icon layer that pops forward, and a cast shadow that grounds the icon back onto the base. I tweaked the timing, distance, and depth of all three relationships until the motion felt right in the body, not just on paper — landing the version that earned leadership sign-off.

Stacking for multi-panel use. Open Store, Chats, Browser, TV — and instead of scattering across space, the panels stack on top of each other. Multi-panel work becomes spatially compact, organized, and easy to recover from.

On/off transitions that breathe. When the Navigator surfaces, the world behind it blurs softly; when it dismisses, the blur fades back in step. The transition is the connection between modes, not a cut between them.

The demo went through multiple rounds of executive and leadership review and was accepted as the visual direction for the next generation of Horizon OS.

3. Navigator People Tab — Motion Iteration

Refining how the People tab inside the Navigator surfaces, focuses, and transitions. Per-tab motion treatments shape what feels like a navigation move versus a state change — small distinctions, big effect on the mental model.

4. Navigator System Bar — Quit from Immersive App

Beyond the Navigator itself: how the system bar handles exiting an immersive experience — preserving spatial context while easing the user back into the OS. The transition is the design.
